📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You can already name the clothes — today you learn to compare them: which is cheaper, which is nicer, which is the best in the store.
In Lessons 6.01–6.02 you brought back the vocabulary of la ropa y las compras. Today is grammar day: no new vocabulary, just one powerful new tool. Real shopping is all comparison — this one costs more, that one is as pretty, this is the best price in the market. By the end you'll build comparisons of inequality (más / menos… que), comparisons of equality (tan… como, tanto… como), and superlatives (el más… de) — plus the four irregulars that native speakers reach for automatically.

⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 6 min

You compare all day — now do it in Spanish

Imagine two jackets on a rack: one costs $80, the other $30. In English you'd say “this one is more expensive than that one.” Spanish uses exactly the same shape — más cara que — where que is the word for “than.” Read this out loud: La chaqueta negra es más cara que la chaqueta gris. You already understand it. Today we make it something you can build on demand.

🧠 PART 3 · GRAMÁTICA · COMPARAR: DESIGUALDAD E IGUALDAD 11 min

More than, less than: comparisons of inequality

To say one thing is more or less than another, wrap it in a simple frame: más / menos + adjective (or noun) + que. That last word, que, means “than.” The adjective still agrees with the first item:

MeaningFrameExample (clothing)
more … thanmás + adj + queLa chaqueta es más cara que la camisa.
less … thanmenos + adj + queEsta falda es menos elegante que el vestido.
more/fewer + noun + thanmás / menos + noun + queTengo más zapatos que mi hermano.

Before a number, “than” becomes de, not que: La camiseta cuesta más de veinte dólares. With a person or thing, it stays que.

Just as much: comparisons of equality

When two things are equal, you have two frames — one for adjectives, one for nouns. With a noun, tanto must agree with it (masculine/feminine, singular/plural):

Use it with…FrameExample (clothing)
an adjectivetan + adj + comoLa camiseta es tan cómoda como el suéter.
a noun (masc. sing.)tanto + noun + comoGasto tanto dinero como tú.
a noun (fem. sing.)tanta + noun + comoCompro tanta ropa como mi prima.
a noun (plural)tantos / tantas + noun + comoTengo tantos zapatos como ella.

Easy way to keep them straight: tan never changes and sits before an adjective; tanto changes to match and sits before a noun (tanto dinero, tanta ropa, tantos zapatos, tantas camisas).

🧠 PART 4 · SUPERLATIVOS, IRREGULARES + PRACTÍCALO 13 min

The most, the least: superlatives

A superlative singles out the “-est” of a group — the cheapest, the prettiest, the best. The frame is el / la / los / las (+ noun) + más / menos + adjective + de, where de means “in” or “of.” The article must match the noun:

FrameExample (clothing)
el/la/los/las + más + adj + deEs la tienda más cara de la ciudad.
el/la/los/las + menos + adj + deSon los zapatos menos caros de la tienda.
el/la + noun + más + adj + deEs el vestido más bonito de la tienda.

Notice the noun sits between the article and más/menos: la tienda más cara = “the most expensive store.” And de la ciudad = “in the city” (Spanish uses de, not en, here).

Four irregulars you should never say the long way

Four adjectives have their own comparative forms — you do not say más bueno or más malo. These same words also serve as the superlative (just add the article):

IrregularMeansNot…Example
mejor(es)better / bestmás buenoEsta camisa es mejor que esa. / Es el mejor precio de la tienda.
peor(es)worse / worstmás maloEsos zapatos son peores que estos.
mayor(es)older / greatermás grande (age)Mi hermana mayor lleva mi talla.
menor(es)younger / lessermás pequeño (age)Mi hermano menor quiere una camiseta nueva.

¡OJO! mejor and peor usually go before the noun (el mejor precio). Use mayor/menor mainly for age; for physical size you can still say más grande / más pequeño (La chaqueta es más grande que el suéter.).

📖 PART 5 · A LEER 5 min

En el mercado de La Paz

En el mercado de La Paz, Bolivia, hay muchísima ropa. La chompa (suéter) de alpaca es más cara que la camiseta de algodón, pero también es mucho más suave y más caliente. Camila mira dos chaquetas.
—Esta chaqueta es tan bonita como esa —dice—, pero cuesta menos. Creo que es la más barata del mercado.
Camila compra tantos regalos como su hermana: dos gorras, una bufanda y un aguayo de colores. Para ella, el aguayo tejido a mano es el recuerdo más bonito de todo el viaje — y, dice, el mejor precio también.

🌎 CULTURA · BOLIVIA
El aguayo: ropa que cuenta una historia

In the Andean highlands of Bolivia, clothing is woven memory. The aguayo (also called awayu) is a rectangular cloth of brilliant stripes that Aymara and Quechua women wear folded across the back to carry babies, market goods, or bundles of potatoes. Its colors and patterns aren't just decoration — in weaving centers like Tarabuco and among the Jalq'a people, the figures woven into each textile encode community identity and stories passed down for generations. The wool often comes from llamas and alpacas, and a finely handwoven piece can take weeks — which is why, in a Bolivian market, the handmade textile is almost always más caro que the machine-made one, and worth it. You'll also spot the cholitas of La Paz in their layered polleras (skirts) and signature bowler hats (el bombín) — once a symbol of exclusion, now worn with open pride. (In the full course this pairs with a short clip of a Tarabuco weaver describing her work.)

★ LA VIRTUD · HONESTY

Jaime Escalante (La Paz, Bolivia, 1930–2010) graded his Garfield High students with unflinching honesty — he never rounded a grade up to spare feelings, because he believed an honest comparison, stated plainly, was the only kind that actually helped a student improve. Today you're learning to make comparisons yourself: más barato, más bonito, el mejor. Practice Escalante's honesty here too — compare prices and quality the way they truly are, not the way that makes your choice sound best.
