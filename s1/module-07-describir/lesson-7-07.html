🧠 PART 3 · EL PRESENTE PROGRESIVO, CONSOLIDADO 8 min

One more look at the whole pattern

You built this across the module: estar (conjugated) + a gerund (-ando for -ar verbs, -iendo for -er/-ir verbs). A handful of common -ir verbs change their stem in the gerund — memorize them as a set.

estarRegular gerundioIrregular gerundio
yo estoyhablando (hablar)leyendo (leer)
tú estáscomiendo (comer)diciendo (decir)
usted/él/ella estáescribiendo (escribir)durmiendo (dormir)
nosotros/as estamosviviendo (vivir)pidiendo (pedir)
ustedes/ellos/ellas estánmirando (mirar)sirviendo (servir)

Quick test: does the action feel caught in the middle of happening? If yes, you want the progressive, not the simple present.

📖 PART 5 · A LEER 5 min

Sofía vive en Montevideo, Uruguay, y habla por video con su prima Renata, que vive en Asunción, Paraguay.

—¡Hola, Renata! ¿Qué estás haciendo ahora mismo?
—Estoy tomando tereré con mi abuela — ella está contando una historia con mucho detalle, y yo estoy escuchando con atención. ¿Y tú, qué estás haciendo?
—Aquí en Montevideo estamos tomando mate, como siempre. Mi hermano todavía está durmiendo — es domingo. Mientras tanto, mi madre está leyendo el periódico lentamente en el patio.
—¡Qué tranquilo! Uruguay y Paraguay son más pequeños que Argentina, pero cada uno tiene su propia manera de tomar las cosas con calma.
—Es verdad. Bueno, sigue describiéndome la historia de tu abuela — quiero oír cada detalle.

🌎 CULTURA · URUGUAY Y PARAGUAY
Dos vecinos pequeños, dos maneras de compartir

Uruguay and Paraguay are two of South America's smaller countries, but each has a ritual built around slowing down and paying attention to the people around you. In Uruguay, almost everyone carries a mate gourd and thermos — sharing it, cup by cup, in a circle is a daily social habit. In Paraguay, the cold version, tereré, serves the same purpose, and Paraguay is also one of the few fully bilingual countries in the Americas: Guaraní, an indigenous language, has equal official status with Spanish, and most Paraguayans speak both. Neither country has a coastline — Paraguay is landlocked entirely, and Uruguay looks out onto the Río de la Plata rather than the open sea — yet both built a national culture of unhurried, attentive conversation. That habit is exactly today's grammar in action: describing, in detail, what is happening right in front of you. (In the full course this pairs with a short listening clip comparing mate and tereré customs.)

★ LA VIRTUD · COURTESY

José Mujica (Uruguay, 1935–2025) was known for speaking to everyone — presidents and passersby alike — in the same calm, respectful tone, never raising his voice or talking down to anyone. That's courtesy expressed through manner, exactly what today's detail-adverbs capture: está hablando cortésmente, está escuchando pacientemente. As you finish your graded work today, notice how a single adverb can turn a plain action into a courteous one.
