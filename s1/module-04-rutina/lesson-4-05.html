Lesson 4.05
★ ¿Con qué frecuencia? — Adverbs of Frequency
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You can already name what you do. Today you learn to say how often you do it.
This is the module's second grammar point, and it's a friendly one — a small set of words called adverbs of frequency (siempre, a veces, nunca, todos los días, una vez por semana…) that answer the question ¿con qué frecuencia? (“how often?”). No new verbs today — you'll keep using the routine verbs you already own. The two moves to master: where the frequency word sits in the sentence, and the two correct ways to say “never” — nunca and no…nunca.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use adverbs and expressions of frequency (siempre, casi siempre, a menudo, a veces, casi nunca, nunca, todos los días, una / dos veces por semana) to say how often something happens.
2
I will be able to place a frequency word correctly — at the start of the sentence, right before the verb, or at the end — without splitting a reflexive pronoun + verb pair.
3
I will be able to say “never” in the two correct ways: nunca before the verb, or no…nunca wrapped around it (the Spanish double negative).
⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 7 min

How often is “often”?

In English you already stretch a sentence with words like always, usually, sometimes, never. Spanish does exactly the same thing, and you've heard most of these words before. Read this aloud and feel how natural it already is: —Yo siempre me despierto temprano, pero casi nunca me levanto enseguida. Today we make that precision yours on purpose.

⭐ PARA IR MÁS LEJOS · optional · +4 min
A quick note on the two “-mente” words: Spanish builds many adverbs the way English adds -ly. General → generalmente, normal → normalmente. That's the same -mente ending you already see on finalmente — a pattern worth recognizing, not memorizing today.
🧠 PART 3 · DÓNDE VA LA PALABRA 10 min

Rule 1 — where the frequency word sits

Spanish is flexible, but there are natural homes for these words. The safest, most native-sounding spots are the start of the sentence or right before the conjugated verb. Time phrases like todos los días also love the very end. The one thing to protect: a reflexive verb keeps its pronoun glued to its front (me levanto), and the frequency word wraps around that pair — it never slips between them.

Word / expressionNatural positionExample (present tense)
siempre / nuncastart, or right before the verbSiempre me despierto temprano. / Nunca llego tarde.
casi siempre / casi nuncastart, or right before the verbCasi siempre desayuno en casa.
generalmente / normalmenteusually the start (or the end)Normalmente me ducho por la mañana.
a menudo / a vecesthe start or the endA veces me acuesto tarde. / Estudio a menudo.
todos los días / cada díathe start or the end (a time phrase)Todos los días me lavo los dientes. / Corro cada día.
una / dos veces por semanathe start or the endVoy al mercado dos veces por semana.

The order that never breaks: pronoun + verb stay together — me levanto, not me siempre levanto. Put the frequency word in front of the pair (siempre me levanto) or after it (me levanto temprano siempre), never inside it.

Rule 2 — two correct ways to say “never”

Here is a place where Spanish and English politely disagree. English forbids the “double negative” (I don't never…). Spanish requires it. If nunca comes before the verb, it stands alone. But if nunca comes after the verb, you must also put no before the verb. Both versions mean exactly the same thing.

StructureExampleMeaning
nunca + verbNunca me acuesto tarde.I never go to bed late.
no + verb … nuncaNo me acuesto tarde nunca.I never go to bed late. (same meaning)

The trap to avoid: you cannot say no me acuesto tarde and mean “never” — that only means “I don't go to bed late.” The word nunca has to be present. And you never need no when nunca already comes first: Nunca me acuesto tarde is complete on its own. The very same pattern works for casi nunca: Casi nunca me acuesto tarde, or No me acuesto tarde casi nunca.

Your turn: say three true sentences about your week — one with siempre, one with a veces, and one “never” sentence (using nunca or no…nunca). Use a reflexive verb in at least one. Say them out loud, then reveal a model.

Siempre me levanto temprano entre semana. A veces me acuesto tarde los viernes. Nunca desayuno en el coche — or, with the double negative, no desayuno en el coche nunca. Yours will use your own habits — the frame is [frecuencia] + (pronombre) + verbo + detalle, with the frequency word wrapped around the verb, never inside me levanto.
📖 PART 5 · A LEER · LA SEMANA DE SOFÍA 5 min

Read it once for the gist, then again and underline every frequency word. Everything is present tense; new place-words are glossed in parentheses.

Sofía vive en Trujillo, una ciudad en la costa norte del Perú. De lunes a viernes siempre se levanta a las seis y media. Casi todos los días desayuna un pan con palta (aguacate) y camina al colegio.

Por la tarde generalmente estudia, y a menudo juega vóley con sus amigas —el vóley es muy popular en el Perú—. Una vez por semana, los sábados, va al mercado con su abuela para comprar fruta fresca.

Entre semana Sofía nunca se acuesta muy tarde. Ella dice: «No me duermo tarde nunca los días de colegio, porque me gusta despertarme con energía». Los domingos, en cambio, la familia casi siempre se reúne para un almuerzo largo, y ese día casi nunca tienen prisa.

📖 ¿COMPRENDES? · interpretive reading
According to the text, how often does Sofía go to the market (al mercado)?
⭐ COMPRUEBA · optional
Find Sofía's sentence with nunca used both ways: «nunca se acuesta muy tarde» (nunca before the verb) and «no me duermo tarde nunca» (the double negative). Two structures, one meaning — exactly Rule 2 from Part 3.
🌎 CULTURA · EL PERÚ
El cebiche: casi siempre a mediodía, casi nunca de noche

Perú's national dish, cebiche — fresh raw fish “cooked” in lime juice with onion and ají — comes with its own rule of frequency built right into the culture. Traditional cevicherías serve it at lunch and casi nunca at dinner, because the fish must arrive that same morning from the boats; by evening it is no longer considered fresh enough. Peruvians will tell you «el cebiche se come de día» — you eat cebiche in the daytime. So a limeño casi siempre orders it at midday and casi nunca after dark: a genuine daily-life habit told entirely in the frequency words you learned today. (In the full course this pairs with a short photo of a coastal cevichería and its lunch-only hours.)

★ LA VIRTUD · SELF-GOVERNMENT

Santa Rosa de Lima (Perú, 1586–1617), patroness of Perú and the first person born in the Americas to be canonized a saint, is remembered for the almost unbreakable regularity of her days — the same hours of prayer, work, and care for the poor of Lima, cada día, for years. That's the quiet secret hiding inside today's grammar: self-government lives in the frequency word. Anyone can rule themselves a veces; real self-government is doing it siempre — todos los días — until the good habit becomes who you are. When you say how often you do something, you're not just describing your week; you're choosing to command it.
