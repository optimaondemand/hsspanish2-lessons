📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Nothing new to learn today — you put the whole module together, and your first two graded tasks open.
Halfway through the module, everything you need is already in your hands: the reflexive routine verbs (me despierto, me ducho, me acuesto), the sequencers that put them in order (primero, luego, después, finalmente), and the adverbs of frequency that say how often (siempre, normalmente, a veces, nunca). Today we weave the three strands into one complete routine — no new grammar, no new words. Then two graded tasks go live: the Vocabulary Check (a listening quiz, 20 pts) and Lengua en acción (a short written routine, 20 pts). This lesson rehearses both before you attempt them.

🧠 PART 3 · LAS TRES PIEZAS EN UNA FRASE 8 min

How the three tools stack in one sentence

Nothing here is new — it's the order that makes a routine sound smooth. Each sentence stacks the same pieces in the same slots: a sequencer to say when, an optional frequency adverb to say how often, then the reflexive verb (pronoun glued to its front) plus a detail like a time.

Sequencer(Frequency)Reflexive verb + detail
Primero,siempreme despierto a las seis.
Luegonormalmenteme levanto y me ducho.
Después,—me visto y desayuno.
Por la noche,a vecesme acuesto tarde.
Finalmente,nuncame duermo con el teléfono.

Two rules you already know, side by side: the pronoun stays glued to its verb (me levanto, never me siempre levanto), and the frequency word wraps around that pair — at the start of the sentence or right before the verb, but never between them. Get those two right and a whole paragraph holds together.

📖 PART 5 · A LEER · UNA RUTINA COMPLETA 6 min

Here's a complete routine that uses all three tools together — a model of exactly what your written task should look like. Read it once for the gist, then again to spot each sequencer, frequency adverb, and reflexive verb. It's set in Arequipa, Perú.

«Me llamo Camila y vivo en Arequipa, una ciudad al sur del Perú. Entre semana siempre me despierto a las seis, porque el colegio empieza temprano. Primero me levanto y abro la ventana — desde mi casa se ve El Misti (un volcán). Luego me ducho rápido y normalmente me visto con el uniforme. Después desayuno pan con queso y a veces un jugo. Por la tarde casi siempre hago la tarea antes de descansar. Por la noche nunca me acuesto muy tarde — finalmente me duermo a las diez, porque mañana el día vuelve a empezar temprano.»

«Mi rutina diaria»

Write a short paragraph (5–6 sentences) describing your own daily routine, following Camila's model above. To earn full marks, include all of these:

🌎 CULTURA · PERÚ
«Allin p’unchay»: la rutina en dos idiomas

In much of the Peruvian Andes, the day doesn't begin in Spanish alone. Millions of Peruvians speak quechua — the language of the Inca, still spoken today — alongside Spanish, and a routine often opens with the Quechua greeting «Allin p’unchay» (“good morning / good day”), answered in Spanish moments later. In Cusco, Puno, and Arequipa you'll hear both woven through the same morning: me levanto, a warm drink, a word of Quechua to a grandparent, a «buenos días» to a neighbor. Naming your own routine in Spanish this week joins you, in a small way, to households that carry two languages through one ordinary day. (In the full course this pairs with a short clip of a bilingual Andean morning.)

★ LA VIRTUD · SELF-GOVERNMENT

Santa Rosa de Lima (Perú, 1586–1617), the first saint of the Americas, is remembered for self-government — the quiet mastery of one's own hours and appetites. She kept a steady, self-chosen rhythm of work, prayer, and care for the poor. Today's lesson is a small echo of that virtue: you're not cramming something new, you're integrating what you already have and choosing to do the graded work well rather than in a rush. A routine you can name and order on purpose — primero, luego, siempre, nunca — is self-government made visible. Steady beats frantic, in Spanish and in life.
