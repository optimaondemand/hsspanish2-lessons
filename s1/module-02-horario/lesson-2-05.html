★ Pido, sirvo, juego — los cambios e→i y u→ue
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two more members of the boot family — and now you can see the whole system at once.
On Day 3 you met the first two “boot” changes: e→ie (empezar→empiezo, querer→quiero) and o→ue (poder→puedo, almorzar→almuerzo). Today we complete the picture with the last two: e→i in verbs like pedir, servir, repetir and seguir, and the one lonely u→ue verb, jugar. Same boot shape, same rule about nosotros — only the vowel is different. No new vocabulary today; we keep using the schedule and everyday verbs you already own.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to conjugate e→i verbs (pedir, servir, repetir, seguir) in the present tense.
2
I will be able to conjugate jugar (u→ue) and explain why nosotros never changes its stem.
3
I will be able to sort any stem-changing verb into its family: e→ie, o→ue, e→i, or u→ue.
⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 6 min

Remember the “boot”

On Day 3 you learned the trick that makes stem-changers easy to picture. Line up the six forms and the ones that change — yo, tú, él/ella, and ellos/ellas — make the shape of a boot. The two forms left outside the boot, nosotros (and vosotros), keep the original vowel. Today's new families follow the exact same boot; only the vowel change is different. Picture the boot before you answer.

🧠 PART 3 · LA GRAMÁTICA: e→i Y u→ue 11 min

First the twist: e→i

Here's the new idea, in plain English. In one group of -ir verbs, the stem e doesn't grow into ie like on Day 3 — it simply tightens to a single i. That's the whole twist: e→i. It happens in exactly the boot forms (yo, tú, él/ella, ellos/ellas) and skips nosotros. Listen to the yo form:

pedir — to ask for / to order

yopidoYo pido el almuerzo a la una.
túpides¿Qué pides en la cafetería?
usted / él / ellapideElla pide una limonada.

ustedes / ellos / ellaspidenEllos piden pupusas.

servir — to serve

💡 ENGLISH SCAFFOLD — THE e→i TWIST

In English, pedir means “to ask for / to order” — think of ordering food (pido pupusas) or asking for a favor. Don't confuse it with preguntar, which means “to ask a question.” And notice the boot: the e becomes i everywhere except nosotros, where it stays e: pedimos, servimos.

Two spelling notes. repetir follows pedir exactly (repito, repites, repite, repetimos, repiten). seguir also changes e→i, but its yo form drops the silent u: sigo (not “siguo”) — then sigues, sigue, seguimos, siguen.

Then the loner: u→ue

There is exactly one verb in Spanish whose u changes to ue in the boot: jugar, “to play” (a sport or game). It behaves just like an o→ue verb — same boot, same skip of nosotros:

SubjectjugarExample
yojuegoYo juego al fútbol después de clase.
tújuegas¿A qué hora juegas?
usted / él / ellajuegaÉl juega en el recreo.
nosotros/asjugamosNosotros jugamos al baloncesto los lunes.
ustedes / ellos / ellasjueganEllas juegan después del almuerzo.

Note: jugar is almost always followed by a before a sport — jugar al fútbol, jugar a las cartas.

—¿Qué haces a la hora del almuerzo?

⭐ COMPRUEBA · optional
Find sigo and seguimos. Why does one have i and the other e? (Answer: sigo is yo — inside the boot, so e→i; seguimos is nosotros — outside the boot, so it keeps the e.) Then find both jugamos and confirm the u stays put in nosotros.
🌎 CULTURA · EL SALVADOR / HONDURAS
Lo que se pide y se sirve: pupusas y baleadas

In El Salvador, when you pides lunch you almost certainly pides a pupusa — a thick hand-made corn tortilla stuffed with cheese, beans, or chicharrón, griddled and servida hot with curtido (a tangy cabbage slaw) and salsa. Salvadorans love them so much that the second Sunday of November is the official Día Nacional de la Pupusa. Cross the border into Honduras and the everyday favorite is the baleada: a warm flour tortilla folded over beans, cream, and cheese. Both are street-corner staples that families and school friends piden, sirven, and happily repiten for seconds. Food vocabulary and today's verbs meet at the same table. (In the full course this pairs with a short menu clip from a pupusería.)

★ LA VIRTUD · COURAGE

Óscar Romero (El Salvador, 1917–1980) repetía — repeated, week after week — his plea for the poor and oppressed of San Salvador from the pulpit, knowing it could cost him his life. He siguió speaking anyway, and was assassinated in 1980 while saying Mass; in 2018 he was declared a saint. His courage, like the verb seguir itself, was not a single act but something he kept doing, again and again.
