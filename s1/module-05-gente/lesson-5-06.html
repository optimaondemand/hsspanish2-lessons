📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you pointed at the thing. Today you point at the person you do it for.
On Day 5 you learned direct object pronouns — lo, la, los, las for the person or thing an action lands on (the what). Today is grammar point 3, a short extension: indirect object pronouns — me, te, le, nos, les — for the person you do something to or for. When you give a gift, send a message, or write a letter, there's a receiver: I write a letter to my friend. That receiver is the indirect object. You'll also meet the little a + noun tag that keeps le and les from being ambiguous. This is a grammar day — no new theme vocabulary, just five pronouns, one clarifier, and a reading set in Chile.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to find the indirect object (the to whom / for whom) and replace it with the right pronoun — me, te, le, nos, or les — placed right before the conjugated verb.
2
I will be able to add the a + noun clarifier (Le escribo a mi abuela) so le and les are never ambiguous — even when the person is already named.
3
I will be able to tell an indirect object pronoun from a direct one — the person you give it to vs. the thing you give — and read both woven through a passage about daily life in Chile.
⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 6 min

Every gift has a receiver

Read this aloud: Yo escribo una carta a mi amiga. Two things get affected here. The letter is what I write — that's the direct object you handled yesterday (la escribo). But my friend is the one I write it to — that's the indirect object, and it gets its own pronoun: Le escribo una carta. English hides this receiver inside little words like “to” and “for”; Spanish gives it a pronoun of its own.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
“I send my grandmother a photo.” Who or what receives the photo — who is it sent to?
📚 PART 2 · LOS CINCO PRONOMBRES · VERBOS DE DAR 8 min

Five pronouns — listen and repeat

This isn't a new theme — it's a small set of grammar words, and the verbs beneath them are ones you already own from earlier this module. Click the 🔊 and say each one aloud. Notice how close they are to the direct set from Day 5: only the third person changes — lo/la/los/las become le and les.

Los pronombres de objeto indirecto

  • meto / for me
  • teto / for you (informal)
  • leto / for him, her, you (formal)
  • nosto / for us
  • lesto / for them, you all

Verbos que suelen llevar un objeto indirecto · giving, sending & telling (repaso)

  • darto give (doy, das, da…)
  • prestarto lend
  • mandarto send
  • escribirto write
  • explicarto explain
⭐ PARA IR MÁS LEJOS · optional · +4 min
Say each verb with a matching pronoun and picture the receiver: me da (gives to me), te presto (I lend to you), le mando (I send to him/her), nos escribe (writes to us), les explico (I explain to them). Feeling the receiver before the verb is the whole habit of this lesson.
🧠 PART 3 · CÓMO FUNCIONAN — LAS DOS IDEAS 11 min

Idea 1 — the pronoun matches the receiver, not a gender

Good news: indirect pronouns are simpler than direct ones. They do not change for masculine or feminine — le covers “to him,” “to her,” and “to you (formal)” alike. You only choose by who receives the action and how many. Like all object pronouns, it sits right before the conjugated verb.

Receiver (to / for whom)PronounIn a sentence
to / for memeMi madre me da dinero.
to / for you (informal)teYo te presto mi libro.
to / for him, her, you (formal)leLe escribo un mensaje.
to / for usnosEl profesor nos explica la tarea.
to / for them, you alllesLes mando fotos.

Same placement rule you learned yesterday: before the conjugated verb (le doy), and with a verb + infinitive you may attach it instead (voy a escribirle = le voy a escribir).

Idea 2 — the a + noun clarifier

Here's the twist: le and les are vague on their own — Le doy un regalo could mean to him, to her, or to you. So Spanish adds a short tag, a + the person, to make it clear. Two things surprise English speakers:

RuleExampleEnglish
Add a + noun to clarify le / lesLe doy un regalo a Valentina.I give Valentina a gift.
Keep the pronoun even when the person is named (doubling)Les escribo a mis abuelos.I write to my grandparents.
Use it for stress too (a mí, a ti)A mí me gusta; ¿y a ti?*I* like it; and you?

That doubling feels redundant in English (“I write to my grandparents to them”), but in Spanish it's completely normal — the pronoun stays and the a + noun spells out who. You've actually seen this pattern before, in gustar: A Ana le gusta la música.

Direct vs. indirect — one question tells them apart

Both sets share me, te, nos. They only split in the third person — and the way to choose is to ask what? vs. to/for whom?

Ask…Direct (the thing/person acted on)Indirect (the receiver)
Question it answers¿qué? — what?¿a quién? / ¿para quién? — to/for whom?
3rd-person pronounslo, la, los, lasle, les
Example¿El regalo? Lo compro. (I buy it.)¿A Valentina? Le compro un regalo. (I buy her one.)

Shortcut: the thing given is direct (lo/la/los/las); the person you give it to is indirect (le/les). We use them one at a time for now — stacking both in one sentence is a next-semester move.

📖 PART 5 · A LEER 5 min

Me llamo Matías y vivo en Valparaíso, un puerto de casas de colores en la costa de Chile. Mi familia está repartida por todo el país largo y delgado: mis abuelos viven en Santiago, la capital, al pie de los Andes. Cada domingo les escribo un mensaje a ellos y siempre me responden con fotos de la cordillera. Mi hermana estudia en el sur, y yo le mando dinero cuando puedo. En el colegio, la profesora nos explica la geografía de Chile: del desierto en el norte a los glaciares en el sur. Por la tarde subo en un ascensor antiguo hasta mi casa; desde arriba veo el mar. Si algún día vienes a Valparaíso, yo te muestro la ciudad.

⭐ COMPRUEBA · optional
Find all five indirect object pronouns in Matías's paragraph (les, me, le, nos, te) and name the receiver of each. Which one is doubled with an a + noun clarifier (les… a ellos)? And why is it le mando dinero but the money itself would be lo? (The sister receives → indirect; the money is the thing given → direct.)
🌎 CULTURA · CHILE
Valparaíso y Santiago: el puerto y la capital

Chile packs its life into a narrow strip between the Andes and the Pacific, and two cities anchor everyday life. Santiago, the capital, sits inland in a valley ringed by snow-capped mountains — on a clear day the cordillera towers over the skyline. About an hour and a half west lies Valparaíso, a UNESCO World Heritage port famous for houses painted every color, steep hills climbed by century-old funiculars called ascensores, and walls covered in murals. The poet Pablo Neruda kept a house here, La Sebastiana, because he loved the view of the bay. Chileans speak a quick, musical Spanish full of their own words — ¿cachai? for “you get it?” and al tiro for “right away.” When Matías writes to his grandparents in Santiago from his hillside in Valparaíso, he's crossing the two poles of ordinary Chilean life: the mountains and the sea. (In the full course this pairs with a short photo tour of the ascensores and a map of Chile north to south.)

★ LA VIRTUD · RESPONSIBILITY

Gabriela Mistral (Chile, 1889–1957), the first Latin American to win the Nobel Prize in Literature, spent years as a teacher writing letters — to students, fellow poets, and governments across the Americas — and took responsibility for making sure each one reached exactly the right person. Today's grammar is a small rehearsal of that same responsibility: when you add a mi abuela after le, you're doing what Mistral did in every letter — taking ownership of who receives your words. Responsibility means the person you're speaking to or for is never left in doubt.
