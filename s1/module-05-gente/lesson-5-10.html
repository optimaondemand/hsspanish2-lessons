🧠 PART 3 · REPASO DE GRAMÁTICA 10 min

The two ideas of this module, side by side

1) Saber vs. conocer — two verbs, one English word. Both mean “to know,” but they split the job. Use saber for facts, information, and know-how; use conocer for being familiar with a person, place, or thing (and for meeting someone for the first time).

Subjectsaberconocer
yoséconozco
túsabesconoces
usted / él / ellasabeconoce
nosotros/assabemosconocemos
ustedes / ellos / ellassabenconocen

Two quick tests: saber + a fact or + an infinitive → Sé tu nombre. Sé nadar.  ·  conocer + a person/place, with the personal a before a person → Conozco a Gabriela. Conozco Santiago.

2) Object pronouns — say it once, then don't repeat the noun. A direct object answers what? or whom? An indirect object answers to/for whom? Both pronouns go right before the conjugated verb (or attach to an infinitive: Voy a comprarlo = Lo voy a comprar).

 Direct object (what/whom)Indirect object (to/for whom)
me / usme / nosme / nos
you (tú)tete
him / her / it / you (Ud.)lo / lale
them / you (Uds.)los / lasles

See the difference in one pair: ¿Tienes las llaves? — Sí, las tengo. (direct: las = the keys) vs. ¿Le das el regalo a tu mamá? — Sí, le doy el regalo. (indirect: le = to your mom). The direct pronoun matches the thing's gender and number; the indirect pronoun only cares whether it's one person (le) or more (les).

📖 PART 5 · A LEER · LAS ONCES EN SANTIAGO 7 min

Read it once for the gist, then again to catch the verbs. Every verb is present tense; watch for conocer/saber and the object pronouns you just reviewed. Culture words are glossed in parentheses.

En Santiago, la capital de Chile, Sofía invita a su nueva vecina, Ignacia, a tomar las onces (an afternoon tea & snack, the Chilean merienda). Sofía no conoce bien a Ignacia todavía, pero sabe que a ella le gusta el té.

—¿Conoces a mi hermano Tomás? —pregunta Sofía.
—No, no lo conozco, pero sé que estudia en la universidad —responde Ignacia.

Sofía prepara pan con palta (avocado) y le sirve una taza de té a Ignacia. —Estas tazas son un regalo de mi abuela —dice—. Las uso solo para las onces. Así, entre el pan y el té, dos vecinas empiezan a conocerse.

📖 COMPRENSIÓN · saber vs. conocer

🌎 CULTURA · CHILE
“Las onces”: la gente y las cosas de la mesa

In Chile, the late-afternoon gathering called las onces (roughly 5–8 p.m.) is a small daily ritual of exactly what this module is about — la gente y las cosas: people around a table with a few beloved things. Chileans drink tea or coffee and share bread with butter, jam, cheese, palta (avocado), or a slice of cake. It's less a meal than an excuse to sit and talk with family, neighbors, and friends. A typical spread reads like this:

— LA MESA DE LAS ONCES —
Pan amasado · Marraqueta · Palta · Mermelada
Té · Café · Manjar (dulce de leche) · Küchen

The word's origin is debated — one popular story says the once (“eleven”) once discreetly stood for the eleven letters of aguardiente. Today it simply means the moment a Chilean home slows down and the neighbors you're just getting to conocer become the friends you already conoces.

★ LA VIRTUD · RESPONSIBILITY

Gabriela Mistral (Chile, 1889–1957) — pen name of Lucila Godoy Alcayaga — was a rural schoolteacher from the Elqui Valley who became, in 1945, the first Latin American writer to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. Her life was built on responsibility: she showed up for students in remote, under-resourced villages and took ownership of their learning when almost no one else would. That's the same responsibility behind today's review: owning your own progress by checking, honestly, what you actually know — saber or conocer, which pronoun and why — before the Grammar Quiz, rather than hoping it goes well.
