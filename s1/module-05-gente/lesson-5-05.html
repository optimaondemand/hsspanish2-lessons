📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Once you've named a thing or a person, you don't have to keep saying it — Spanish gives you a tiny word that stands in for it.
Listen to yourself in English: “I have the keys. Do you need the keys? I'll bring the keys.” After the first time, we swap in a pronoun — “I have them.” Spanish does exactly the same with direct object pronouns: lo, la, los, las for things and people already named, plus me, te, nos for me, you, and us. This is the module's second grammar point, and it works on the very words you already own from Days 1–2 — las cosas (the keys, the charger, the headphones) and la gente (your neighbor, your classmate). No new vocabulary today — just a smarter, less repetitive way to talk about the words you already have.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to replace a thing or person I already named with the direct object pronoun that agrees in gender and number — lo, la, los, or las.
2
I will be able to use me, te, and nos to say that someone knows, sees, or helps me, you, or us.
3
I will be able to place the pronoun correctly — before a conjugated verb, or attached to the end of an infinitive.
⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 6 min

Stop repeating yourself

Read this little exchange out loud, in English: “—Do you have your headphones? —Yes, I have my headphones. I always carry my headphones.” By the third time, your ear rebels — nobody talks like that. We'd say “Yes, I have them. I always carry them.” That little word them is a direct object pronoun. Spanish leans on the same trick, and today you'll learn its four flavors for things — lo, la, los, las — plus me, te, nos for people.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
In “I always carry them,” the word them stands in for something already mentioned. What did it replace?

🧠 PART 3 · LOS PRONOMBRES DE OBJETO DIRECTO 10 min

The full set — and where the pronoun goes

First, what does “direct object” mean? It's the thing or person that receives the action — the answer to “what?” or “whom?” after the verb. Tengo las llaves → I have what? the keys. Conozco a Camila → I know whom? Camila. Once you've said it, you can replace it with one of these:

PronounStands for…Example
memeMi vecina me ayuda.
teyou (informal)Yo te conozco bien.
lohim / it (masculine)¿El cargador? Lo tengo.
laher / it (feminine)¿La mochila? La llevo.
nosusEl profesor nos conoce.
losthem (masculine / mixed)¿Los audífonos? Los comparto.
lasthem (feminine)¿Las llaves? Las devuelvo.

The four “thing” pronouns must agree with the noun they replace: match its gender (m/f) and its number (singular/plural). El cargador is masculine singular → lo. Las llaves is feminine plural → las. The same lo/la/los/las also replace people in the third person: Veo a Diego → Lo veo. Veo a Camila → La veo.

Noun → pronoun: watch the swap

Full sentenceReplace…With the pronoun
Presto el cargador.el cargador (m. sing.)Lo presto.
Devuelvo la cartera.la cartera (f. sing.)La devuelvo.
Comparto los audífonos.los audífonos (m. pl.)Los comparto.
Tengo las llaves.las llaves (f. pl.)Las tengo.
Conozco a mi vecina.a mi vecina (person, f.)La conozco.

Where does it go?

This is the one placement rule to hold on to — and it's short:

When the verb is…The pronoun goes…Example
a single conjugated verbright before itLas tengo. / No lo conozco.
a conjugated verb + infinitive
(voy a…, quiero…, tengo que…, puedo…)		either before the first verb or attached to the end of the infinitive — both are correctVoy a prestarlo. = Lo voy a prestar.

Two details: (1) In a negative sentence, no comes first, then the pronoun: No las tengo. (2) When you attach the pronoun to an infinitive, it becomes one word — devolver + las → devolverlas. Everything else you already know stays the same; you're only adding a two-letter word in the right spot.

Ask two questions each time: what gender and number is the noun? (that picks lo/la/los/las) and where does the pronoun go? Pick the best answer, read the feedback, and Reset to try again.

✅ TRY IT · pick the pronoun for a thing

✅ TRY IT · agree in gender AND number

✅ TRY IT · attach it to the infinitive

Your turn: your classmate asks «¿Me prestas los audífonos y la mochila?» Answer “Yes, I'll lend them and I'll share it too” — using a pronoun for each thing. Say it out loud first, then reveal a model.

Sí, los presto y la comparto también. Los audífonos is masculine plural → los; la mochila is feminine singular → la; each pronoun sits right before its conjugated verb. (You could also add a second pronoun — «te los presto» — and you'll do exactly that officially in 5.06.)
📖 PART 5 · A LEER 5 min

—Oye, Camila, ¿tienes mi paraguas? Llueve otra vez en Valparaíso.
—Sí, lo tengo en mi mochila. Te lo devuelvo ahora.
—Gracias. Y las llaves del edificio, ¿las tienes tú también?
—No, no las tengo. Creo que las tiene doña Rosa — ya sabes, la vecina del primer piso.
—Ah, ¡claro! La conozco bien; siempre nos ayuda. Voy a buscarla.
—Perfecto. Y tus audífonos… ¿me los prestas para el bus?
—¡Claro que sí! Los comparto contigo. Para eso somos compañeros.

⭐ COMPRUEBA · optional
Find every lo, la, los, las and say what each one replaces (the umbrella? the keys? doña Rosa?). Then spot the one attached to an infinitive — buscarla — and notice how it could just as well be «la voy a buscar.» Same meaning, both correct.
🌎 CULTURA · CHILE
Valparaíso y sus ascensores

The port city of Valparaíso climbs more than forty steep hills (cerros), and since the late 1800s residents have ridden up and down them on ascensores — funicular railways that are half elevator, half tiny mountain train. Several are over 120 years old and still running; the historic quarter they serve is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Around them, the hillsides are famous for open-air murales that turn whole staircases and walls into art. To really know Valparaíso you have to conocer it on foot — ride an ascensor, get lost among the murals, and you'll understand why Chileans say the city has to be walked, not just described. (In the full course this pairs with a short photo tour of the ascensor Concepción and a mural gallery.)

★ LA VIRTUD · RESPONSIBILITY

Gabriela Mistral (Chile, 1889–1957) — teacher, diplomat, and the first Latin American to win the Nobel Prize in Literature (1945) — took responsibility for being understood, choosing every word so her reader was never left guessing. Today's grammar asks the same of you: a pronoun like lo or las only works if your listener can tell what it replaces, so using it well is a responsibility you owe the person you're speaking to. Trim the repetition, but never at the cost of clarity — that trade-off is yours to manage.
