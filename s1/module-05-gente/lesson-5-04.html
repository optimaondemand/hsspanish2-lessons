★ ¿Saber o conocer? — el verbo correcto en contexto
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
English uses one word — know — for two very different ideas. Today you decide, out loud, which Spanish verb each one needs.
Yesterday you met saber and conocer, the two verbs Spanish uses where English just says to know. Today there is no new grammar and no new vocabulary — we take the rule you already have and put it to work. You'll choose between the two verbs in real situations, notice the yo-irregular forms sé and conozco in action, and finish with a short reading from Chile where both verbs appear naturally, describing people and places.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to choose saber or conocer depending on whether I mean a fact/skill or being familiar with a person or place.
2
I will be able to use the yo-irregular forms sé and conozco correctly in my own sentences.
3
I will be able to read a short text and explain why each saber or conocer was the right choice.
⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 7 min

Two kinds of “knowing”

Think about how you “know” your best friend versus how you “know” your phone number. You know your friend by being familiar with them — that's conocer. You know your phone number as a fact you could recite — that's saber. English blurs the two; Spanish keeps them apart. Read this aloud: —Sé su número, pero no conozco muy bien a su familia.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Which of these do you “know” mostly by being familiar with it over time — rather than as a fact you could write down?
📚 PART 2 · ESCÚCHALO OTRA VEZ 8 min

The forms you already met — listen and repeat

Nothing new here — these are the two verbs from yesterday. Click the 🔊 and say each one aloud so the forms are on the tip of your tongue before you start choosing between them. Notice that the only surprise is the yo form of each verb (sé, conozco); everything else is regular.

  • (yo) séI know (a fact / how to)
  • (tú) sabesyou know (a fact / how to)
  • (él/ella) sabehe/she knows (a fact / how to)
  • (yo) conozcoI know / am familiar with
  • (tú) conocesyou know / are familiar with
  • (él/ella) conocehe/she knows / is familiar with
⭐ PARA IR MÁS LEJOS · optional · +3 min
Say two quick sentences with the yo forms: Sé nadar (a skill → saber + infinitive) and Conozco Santiago (a place → conocer). Both use “I,” but the verb changes because the kind of knowing changes.
🧠 PART 3 · LA REGLA DE DECISIÓN 8 min

One question decides it

Here's the whole rule again, as a decision you make in a second. Before you pick a verb, ask: “Is this a fact/skill, or a person/place I'm familiar with?”

Use this verb……when you meanExample
sabera fact or piece of informationSé dónde está el museo.
saber + infinitivohow to do something (a skill)Mi hermana sabe tocar la guitarra.
conocer + personabeing acquainted with a personConozco a Camila.
conocer + lugar/cosabeing familiar with a place or thingElla conoce Valparaíso muy bien.

Two quick reminders you already know: with saber, an infinitive right after it means “know how to” (sé cocinar). With conocer, when the thing you know is a person, add the personal a (conozco a Gabriela). No new rule — just the same two verbs, applied.

🧠 PART 4 · PRACTÍCALO 12 min
✅ TRY IT · person or fact?
“Yo ___ a la profesora nueva.” (I know / am acquainted with the new teacher.)
✅ TRY IT · a piece of information
“¿___ tú dónde está el museo?” (Do you know where the museum is?)
✅ TRY IT · a skill
“Mi hermano ___ tocar la guitarra.” (My brother knows how to play the guitar.)

Your turn: say two true sentences about yourself — one with sé (a fact or a skill) and one with conozco (a person or a place). Say them out loud first, then reveal a model.

Sé tocar el piano y sé dónde vive mi mejor amiga. Conozco a mi vecino y conozco bien el centro de mi ciudad. Notice: sé covers the skill (tocar) and the fact (dónde vive); conozco covers the person (with personal a) and the place (no a).
📖 PART 5 · A LEER 5 min

Me llamo Camila y vivo en Valparaíso, en la costa de Chile. Conozco muy bien mi ciudad: conozco todas las calles empinadas y los cerros con sus ascensores famosos. También sé que Valparaíso es Patrimonio de la Humanidad, y sé llegar al puerto sin mapa. Conozco a muchos artistas del barrio — pintan murales enormes en las paredes. No sé el nombre de todos, pero conozco a Don Pedro, que vende empanadas cerca de mi casa. Y ¿tú? ¿Sabes dónde está Valparaíso? ¿Conoces alguna ciudad chilena?

⭐ COMPRUEBA · optional
Camila uses conocer for her city, its streets, and the artists (places and people), and saber for facts and for “knowing how” to reach the port (information and a skill). Find one conocer that takes the personal a — and explain why. (Hint: it's the one about a person.)
🌎 CULTURA · CHILE
Valparaíso y sus ascensores

Valparaíso, Chile's historic port city, climbs up more than forty steep cerros (hills) that ring a natural bay. To move people between the flat port and the neighborhoods above, the city built dozens of ascensores — funicular railways — starting in the 1880s; several still run today. The tangle of colorful houses, staircases, and street murals earned the old town a place on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2003. To really conocer Valparaíso, locals say, you have to walk (and ride) its hills — you can saber facts about it from a book, but you only conocer it by being there. That contrast is exactly the one you practiced today. (In the full course this pairs with a short photo-and-map task — Un paseo por los cerros.)

★ LA VIRTUD · RESPONSIBILITY

Gabriela Mistral (Chile, 1889–1957) — poet, rural schoolteacher, and diplomat — became in 1945 the first Latin American writer to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. She began teaching as a teenager in small mountain villages and took full responsibility for her students' real needs, not merely the facts about them. That's responsibility in action: knowing when a situation calls for saber (a fact) and when it calls for conocer (a person), and choosing the right verb — and the right care — for each. Today's decision rule is really a small exercise in taking that responsibility seriously.
