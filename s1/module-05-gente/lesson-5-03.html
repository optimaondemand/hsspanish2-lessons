★ Dos maneras de “saber”: saber vs. conocer
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
English gives you one word for “to know.” Spanish gives you two — and the difference is worth getting exactly right.
You've spent two days gathering the people and things of this module. Today comes the module's first grammar point, and it's a famous one: Spanish splits the English verb “to know” into saber and conocer. One is for facts, information, and skills (things you carry in your head); the other is for familiarity — being acquainted with people, places, and things you've experienced firsthand. We'll teach the contrast in English first, line up both conjugations, and pay special attention to their surprising yo-forms: sé and conozco. No new vocabulary today — we put Days 1–2 words to work.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain when Spanish uses saber (facts, information, and how to do something) versus conocer (being familiar with a person, place, or thing).
2
I will be able to conjugate saber and conocer in the present tense, including their irregular yo-forms sé and conozco.
3
I will be able to build true sentences with saber + an infinitive and conocer + a + a person, using this module's people and things.
⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 6 min

Two kinds of “knowing”

Read these two English sentences slowly: “I know the answer” and “I know my neighbor.” Same word — but they aren't the same kind of knowing. The first is a fact in your head; the second is a relationship, a familiarity built over time. Spanish keeps these two ideas in two different verbs. Feel that difference now and the whole lesson clicks into place.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Which of these “knowings” feels most like a fact you could write down, and which feels more like being familiar with someone? Pick whichever draws your eye — you'll sort them precisely in Part 3.
📚 PART 2 · ESCUCHA LOS DOS VERBOS 7 min

Hear them before you use them

These aren't new words — you met both in Spanish 1 — but today they're the stars, so tune your ear first. Click the 🔊 and repeat each aloud, especially the two yo-forms, which don't sound the way you'd guess.

  • saberto know (facts, information, how to…)
  • (yo) séI know (irregular yo-form)
  • conocerto know (be familiar with people, places, things)
  • (yo) conozcoI know / I'm familiar with (irregular yo-form, -zco)
⭐ ESCUCHA CON CUIDADO · optional · +2 min
Notice sé carries a written accent (tilde) — it's what separates the verb sé (“I know”) from the little word se. And conozco hides a hard sound you can hear: co—NOZ—co. Say both three times until they feel automatic.
🧠 PART 3 · LA GRAMÁTICA 10 min

Which “to know” do I need?

Here's the whole rule in one line, in English: use saber for facts, information, and how to do something; use conocer for being personally familiar with people, places, and things. This table sorts it out — read the “test” column and the idea sticks.

Use…when you mean…Quick test & example
sabera fact or piece of information“know that / who / where / when” → Sé dónde están las llaves.
saber + infinitivea skill — knowing how to do something“know how to ___” → Mi compañera sabe tocar la guitarra.
conocerbeing familiar with a personmeet / be acquainted → Conozco a mi vecino.
conocerbeing familiar with a place or thinghave been there / experienced it → ¿Conoces Santiago?

Now the forms. Both are regular in five of six present-tense endings — the only curveball is the yo-form of each (highlighted). Everything else follows the ordinary -er pattern you already own.

Subjectsaber (facts / how-to)conocer (familiarity)
yosé ← irregularconozco ← irregular (-zco)
túsabesconoces
usted / él / ellasabeconoce
nosotros/assabemosconocemos
ustedes / ellos / ellassabenconocen

One small partner rule: when conocer is followed by a specific person, add the “personal a” — Conozco a mi jefa. (You already saw this a in Spanish 1.) No a is needed with places or things: Conozco Santiago. Hear the contrast in one breath: Sé que mi jefa es exigente (a fact) versus Conozco a mi jefa (I'm acquainted with her).

🧠 PART 4 · PRACTÍCALO 12 min

For each one, ask the test question first: Is this a fact/skill (saber) or a familiarity with a person/place/thing (conocer)? The people and things come straight from Days 1–2. Commit to an answer, read the feedback, and use Reset if you want another try.

✅ TRY IT · fact or familiarity?
“Yo ___ a mi vecina doña Rosa; vive en mi edificio.” (I know her — I'm familiar with her as a person.)
✅ TRY IT · pick the right verb
“Mi compañero es muy trabajador y ___ arreglar cualquier aparato.” (he knows how to fix any gadget — a skill.)
✅ TRY IT · the yo-form
“Yo no ___ dónde está mi cargador.” (I don't know where my charger is — it's a piece of information.)

Your turn: say two true sentences out loud — one with sé (a fact or a skill you have) and one with conozco a + a real person from your life (use a word from Day 1 if you like: mi vecino, mi colega, mi pareja). Then reveal a model.

Sé tocar el piano y sé que mi colega es muy amable. Conozco a mi vecina; es sincera y generosa. The first uses sé for a skill (sé tocar) and a fact (sé que…); the second uses conozco + the personal a for a person. Swap in your own people and skills — the frames are Sé + [hecho / infinitivo] and Conozco a + [persona].
📖 PART 5 · A LEER 5 min

Me llamo Tomás y vivo en Valparaíso. Conozco a casi todos mis vecinos, pero hay un desconocido nuevo en el tercer piso: no lo conozco todavía y no sé cómo se llama. Mi compañera Antonia, que es muy amable, sí sabe su nombre — y además sabe reparar computadoras, así que mañana le presto mi aparato. Ella conoce bien el barrio y conoce Santiago también. «¿Sabes dónde queda el mercado?», le pregunto. «Claro — y conozco al vendedor. Es muy sincero.»

⭐ COMPRUEBA · optional
Find one use of saber for a fact, one for a skill (saber + infinitive), and one use of conocer for a person and one for a place. Why does Tomás say he does not conocer the new neighbor — and does not saber his name?
🌎 CULTURA · CHILE
«¿Cachái?» — the very Chilean way to say “you know?”

In everyday Chilean speech you'll hear one little word constantly: «cachái» — roughly “you get it? / you know?” It comes from the English verb to catch (“do you catch my meaning?”) and is uniquely Chilean; a friend from Mexico or Spain might not use it at all. Chileans tuck it onto the end of sentences the way English speakers say “you know?” — «Está lejos, cachái». It's a fitting note for today: “knowing” runs all through how people actually talk, and every Spanish-speaking country flavors it differently. To truly conocer Chile is to catch its slang — something no verb chart alone can teach you. (In the full course this pairs with a short clip of Chilean street conversation — count the «cachái»s.)

★ LA VIRTUD · RESPONSIBILITY

Gabriela Mistral (Chile, 1889–1957) — rural teacher, poet, and the first Latin American to win the Nobel Prize in Literature (1945) — took responsibility for genuinely knowing each of her students, not just the facts on their record. That's the difference this lesson opens: you can saber a person's name and address and still owe them the deeper responsibility of conocer — of actually knowing them. Responsibility toward people starts with paying real attention to who they are, not just what's written about them.
