★ Yo conozco — when only yo changes
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
One tiny sound change, and only in the yo form — everything else you already know stays exactly the same.
A whole family of verbs whose infinitive ends in -cer or -cir does something quirky: in the yo form the c becomes -zco. So conocer → yo conozco, conducir → yo conduzco, ofrecer → yo ofrezco. The good news: tú, él, nosotros, ellos — all completely regular. Our anchor verb is conocer, “to be acquainted with” people and places — the verb you reach for to say who and where you know.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to form the yo ending -zco on conocer, conducir, and ofrecer (conozco, conduzco, ofrezco).
2
I will be able to keep every other person (tú, él/ella, nosotros, ellos/ellas) fully regular.
3
I will be able to use conocer to say which people and places I am acquainted with.
⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 6 min

You already know most of this verb

In Spanish 1 you met conocer next to saber: conocer is knowing in the sense of being acquainted with — a person, a city, a team. Read this aloud: —¿Conoces a Mariano Rivera? —Sí, yo conozco su historia. Notice how conoces (you) looks totally normal — and conozco (I) sneaks in an extra z. That single change is the whole lesson.

🧠 PART 3 · LA GRAMÁTICA — VERBOS -ZCO 12 min

The pattern: c → -zco, but only in yo

When a verb ends in a vowel + -cer or vowel + -cir, its yo form swaps the c for -zco. Think of it as the same “only-yo-is-weird” habit you saw with the -go verbs (tengo, hago) — a different sound, but the same rule: one form changes, the rest behave. Click the 🔊 on each yo form and say it back:

  • conocerto be acquainted with (people/places)  →  yo conozco
  • conducirto drive  →  yo conduzco
  • ofrecerto offer  →  yo ofrezco

Here is the full paradigm for our anchor verb, conocer. Look how only the first row is special — the rest are ordinary -er endings:

SubjectconocerExample
yoconozco  ← irregularYo conozco a todo el equipo.
túconoces¿Conoces la cancha nueva?
usted / él / ellaconoceElla conoce bien Panamá.
nosotros/asconocemosNosotros conocemos al entrenador.
ustedes / ellos / ellasconocenEllos conocen a los jugadores.

See it? Yo = conozco; everyone else keeps the plain -er endings (-es, -e, -emos, -en). One row changes, four rows stay home.

The same pattern carries the other two verbs — only the yo form is different:

💡 PISTA · personal a
When you use conocer with a person, add the little a: Conozco a Mariano. With a place, no a is needed: Conozco Panamá. You already used this personal a in Spanish 1 — here it is again.

📖 PART 5 · A LEER 5 min

Me llamo Sofía y vivo en la Ciudad de Panamá. Yo conozco a muchos deportistas porque mi papá es entrenador de béisbol. Cada sábado yo conduzco con él a la cancha — bueno, todavía no tengo licencia, ¡pero pronto! En el equipo, yo siempre ofrezco ayuda con el agua y los bates. Mi hermano conoce a los jugadores mayores, y nosotros conocemos cada rincón del estadio. Para mí, el béisbol no es solo un partido: es mi familia.

⭐ COMPRUEBA · optional
Find the three yo forms that end in -zco (conozco, conduzco, ofrezco) and the two forms that do not change (conoce, conocemos). Why does Sofía say conozco but her brother conoce? Same verb — different subject, so only yo takes the -zco.
🌎 CULTURA · PANAMÁ
El béisbol, la pasión del istmo

Panamá sits at the narrow bridge between two continents — the same geography that made the Canal de Panamá one of the world's great crossroads. Its national sporting passion isn't soccer but béisbol, carried in through the Canal Zone over a century ago and now woven into everyday life. Panama has sent dozens of players to the U.S. major leagues from a country of just over four million people — among them Rod Carew and Mariano Rivera, names any Panamanian fan conoce by heart. On a Saturday you'll find neighborhood canchas full of kids who dream of the same path. (In the full course this pairs with a short listening clip — Voces del estadio.)

★ LA VIRTUD · PERSEVERANCE

Mariano Rivera (Panamá, b. 1969) grew up in the fishing town of Puerto Caimito, catching a ball with a glove made from a milk carton and a bat carved from tree branches. He was signed for a few thousand dollars, spent years grinding through the minor leagues, and refined a single pitch — the cutter — until it became the most feared in baseball. In 2019 he became the first player ever elected to the Hall of Fame unanimously. His story is perseverancia itself: not one dramatic leap, but thousands of ordinary repetitions. Learning a language works the same way — today's -zco forms become effortless the same way Rivera's cutter did: one honest practice at a time.
